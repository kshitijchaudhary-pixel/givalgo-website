We are one week away from Giving Tuesday - the starting gun for the biggest five weeks of philanthropic activity of the year. On December 1, an estimated 36 million donors will open their wallets. By December 31, foundations will have rushed to disburse their final grants of the year. DAF sponsors will process their highest recommendation volume. And nonprofits across the country will have stood in front of more new donors and funders in one month than they will for the rest of the year combined.

That concentration of activity is an opportunity. It is also when things go wrong.

Grantmakers under pressure to close out the fiscal year cut corners on due diligence. Charities scramble to respond to new funders without having updated their public records in months. Donors give generously to organizations whose IRS status lapsed two years ago. The five-week window between Giving Tuesday and December 31 is when both sides of the giving equation most need to be prepared, and when both sides are most likely not to be.

This piece is a prep list for both. If you are a grantmaker, section one is for you. If you run or work at a nonprofit, section two is for you. If you do both - if you are a foundation that also fundraises, or a fiscal sponsor - read both.

For grantmakers: the year-end compliance crunch

December is when foundations disburse more grant dollars than any other month. The pressure to meet annual giving targets, combined with board meeting cycles that concentrate final approval in November and December, means that more money moves in the last four weeks of the year than the preceding eight combined at many foundations.

Speed is not inherently a problem. But speed without verification is.

Our analysis of Form 990 grant schedules for tax year 2023 found roughly 4,000 grants that went to organizations the IRS had already revoked. Combined value: approximately $45 million. Median grant size: $200. These are not anomalies. They are the predictable result of grantmakers working from grantee lists built months or years ago, without checking whether the organizations on those lists have changed.

An IRS status can be revoked silently. The nonprofit doesn't get a phone call. The organization continues to operate. Its website stays up. Its staff keeps showing up. And its funders keep sending money, unaware that the charitable deduction they are offering their donors no longer applies.

The IRS updates its Business Master File, the definitive list of active tax-exempt organizations, monthly. By this time of year, the version in your grants management system may not reflect organizations that lost status in October or November. That gap is where compliance risk lives.

DAF sponsors face this problem at scale. The week of Giving Tuesday is when DAF account holders, flush from watching their peers give publicly, submit their highest volume of grant recommendations. A large DAF sponsor processing thousands of recommendations in a 72-hour window faces the same verification problem that individual foundations face, compacted into a fraction of the time. In 2024, US DAF programs held $326 billion in assets and distributed over $54 billion in grants. Even a small error rate on that volume translates to millions of dollars disbursed to ineligible recipients.

The DAF timing problem: Most DAF account holders assume the sponsor screens every recommendation for IRS eligibility and sanctions. Many sponsors do, but the check is often run once at account opening, not at the moment of each individual grant recommendation. A nonprofit that passed a check eighteen months ago may not pass one today.

The pre-disbursement checklist for year-end grants

  • IRS active status. Confirm the EIN appears in the current IRS Business Master File as tax-exempt. The BMF is updated monthly; your GMS may be running on a stale version. Cross-check directly at IRS.gov or via a service that syncs to the live file.
  • Auto-revocation list. Run every grantee's EIN against the IRS Auto-Revocation List. Over 275,000 organizations have lost status for failing to file for three consecutive years. Many have not been reinstated. Appearing on this list disqualifies a grant from being tax-deductible.
  • OFAC and sanctions screening. Check the organization and its key personnel against the OFAC Specially Designated Nationals list and other applicable sanctions databases. This is a legal requirement for certain funders, and a best practice for all.
  • State charitable registration. Verify the grantee is currently registered to solicit in the states where they operate. Lapsed registrations are common and are a signal of administrative strain, which often precedes larger organizational problems.
  • 990 recency and financial health. Review the most recently available Form 990. Check the program expense ratio (median is around 87% for the sector), reserves, and whether revenue has been declining. For year-end grants, pay particular attention to whether the organization has been operating at a deficit.
  • Website and mission alignment. Does the grantee's current website tell the same story as their 990? A divergence - a program listed in the 990 that has been quietly discontinued, or a new major initiative the foundation didn't fund - is worth a conversation before disbursement.
  • Leadership continuity. A new executive director or significant board turnover in the past 12 months changes the relationship and may change the organization's direction. This is especially worth checking for multi-year grants where the original relationship was with a predecessor.
  • Adverse news and legal records. Run a news search for the organization and its key leaders. Legal actions, regulatory notices, and media coverage of financial irregularities are publicly available and rarely appear in a foundation's internal records until after a problem has become a scandal.

For nonprofits: what donors and funders see when they look you up

Giving Tuesday puts your organization in front of more first-time donors and funders in a single day than most nonprofits see in a quarter. The ones most likely to become major donors, and the foundations most likely to fund you, will look you up before they give.

What they find is not controlled by you in real time. It is a composite of your most recent Form 990, your nonprofit profiles on platforms like Givalgo, Candid/GuideStar, and Charity Navigator, your state charity registration status, and whatever comes up first in a Google search. That composite may reflect your organization as it existed twelve or eighteen months ago, not as it exists today.

A calendar-year nonprofit's most recent publicly searchable 990 can be 18 months old or more, once you account for the filing deadline (November 15 for most) and the lag before it appears in IRS databases. A donor giving on December 1, 2026 may be reading a 990 from early 2025.

You cannot retroactively file a better 990 for the giving season that starts next week. What you can do is make sure the information that is under your control - your website, your program descriptions, your state registrations, and your profiles on philanthropy data platforms - is current, accurate, and consistent with the 990 a funder will find.

Inconsistency is what catches a funder's attention, and not in a good way. A website that describes three active programs and a 990 that shows only one is not fraud. But it looks like disorganization - or at minimum, like an organization that is not watching its own public record. In a season when funders are making quick decisions under time pressure, disorganization is disqualifying.

21% of US donors give exclusively in November and December. Those donors are evaluating organizations they may never have heard of before. The question is not whether they will research you: some will, some won't. The question is whether the picture they find is accurate.

The pre-giving-season checklist for nonprofits

  • Confirm your IRS status. Look up your own EIN at IRS.gov. Confirm you appear in the Business Master File as currently tax-exempt and that you are not on the Auto-Revocation List. This should take five minutes. If you are on the revocation list, addressing it now, not in January, is essential.
  • Claim and update your nonprofit profiles. Givalgo, Candid/GuideStar, and Charity Navigator are the first places a foundation program officer looks after Googling your name. Claimed profiles let you add current program information, leadership, and financials that go beyond what's in the 990. An unclaimed or stale profile is a missed opportunity - and a signal to funders that the organization is not paying attention to how it presents itself. Some of this, including governance policies and program descriptions, you can update today, regardless of your 990 filing status.
  • Verify state charitable registration in every state you fundraise. If you are running a Giving Tuesday campaign and soliciting donations from residents of multiple states, you are legally required to be registered in most of them. Lapsed registrations are a liability, and they appear in public records that diligent funders check.
  • Align your website with your 990. Read your most recently filed 990 alongside your current website. If you have added, changed, or discontinued programs since the filing, your website should reflect the current reality, and your program staff should be ready to explain the evolution to a funder who has read the 990.
  • Update your program descriptions for discovery. Giving Tuesday is when foundations and DAF account holders are most actively looking for new grantees. Databases that surface grantee opportunities, including Givalgo Discover, pull from 990 program descriptions and other public records. If your program language is stale or generic, you may not appear in searches that you should appear in.
  • Find the right funders to approach. Rather than waiting for inbound interest, use this week to identify foundations that have a track record of funding causes like yours. Look for funders who have supported organizations similar to yours in size, mission, and geography, and in a similar grant range to your typical ask. Givalgo Discover lets you search by program area, region, historical grant size, and grantee profile to surface foundations actively grantmaking in your space. A timely, targeted outreach to five well-matched funders is worth more than a mass email to fifty generic ones.
  • Prepare a one-paragraph organizational summary. When a foundation program officer gets your Giving Tuesday email or sees your campaign, they may forward it to a colleague with one line of context. Give them that line yourself: a crisp, accurate description of what you do, who you serve, and what distinguishes your approach. This is not a tagline - it is the paragraph that travels without you.

The five-week window is shared

The due diligence problem for grantmakers and the credibility problem for nonprofits are two sides of the same five-week window. When a nonprofit's public records are current and accurate, a grantmaker can verify them quickly and disburse confidently. When a grantmaker has real-time monitoring in place, a nonprofit gets paid faster because there are no delays waiting for manual verification.

The organizations that move the most money this giving season, with the fewest problems, will be the ones where both sides of the transaction showed up prepared. That preparation does not require a large team or a large budget. It requires checking the things that are publicly available and making sure the information you control is accurate.

The IRS does not send reminders when it revokes a nonprofit's status. Charity Navigator does not call when a rating drops. Foundations do not explain why a grant was declined. The five-week window does not wait.
