On October 26, Anthropic made its IPO filing public. One line in it got more attention than the revenue figures: the company’s seven cofounders “have each pledged to dedicate 80% of their personal equity in Anthropic to philanthropic causes.” Fortune estimated the pledges are worth about $79 billion at the company’s latest private valuation, roughly $11 billion per founder.

Two caveats matter. The filing calls the pledges “voluntary and revocable,” and it leaves both the timing and the recipients open. A pledge isn’t a grant. Still, it is one of the clearest signals yet that a new class of donors, made rich by AI, is about to enter philanthropy, and that it plans to move a lot of money.

It isn’t just one company

The Anthropic filing joins a run of AI-linked commitments this year:

  • Anthropic employees. The company says it will match staff donations of company shares, which employees have pledged in amounts worth billions of dollars, according to CNBC. Fortune separately estimates the company’s own public-benefit commitment at about $50 billion at the same valuation.
  • The OpenAI Foundation is distributing $50 million to 163 community nonprofits through its 2026 People-First AI Fund, with every grant to be paid out before year-end.
  • Coefficient Giving raised its pledge to $1 billion, explicitly counting on an AI windfall.
  • Humanity AI, a coalition of foundations including MacArthur, Ford and Mellon, has committed $500 million to shaping how AI affects people and communities.

Projections of the total vary widely, from tens of billions a year to several hundred billion over time, depending on whose valuation you trust. But the direction is clear.

Where the money lands first: donor-advised funds

Most of this wealth won’t arrive as a new foundation with a program staff. It will arrive through donor-advised funds (DAFs). The Anthropic filing names two: Daffy Charitable Fund and Dechomai Asset Trust, which have “master donation” agreements to receive share donations from the company and its employees.

Their Form 990 filings show how fast this channel is growing:

Daffy is built for digital-native donors who want to give from an app. Dechomai specializes in hard-to-value assets, such as private company stock, that most charities can’t accept directly. Together they look like the new donor: wealthy on paper, giving through a platform, and recommending grants with no foundation staff behind them.

Why this matters: DAFs have no annual payout requirement. Money can sit in a fund for years, or move in a week. When a large group of donors gets liquidity at once, as an IPO lock-up expiry would provide, a lot of grant recommendations can arrive in a short window.

Four shifts grantmakers should prepare for

1. Speed will outrun diligence

New donors who made their money in fast-moving companies tend to expect giving to move just as fast. The bottleneck won’t be dollars. It will be the capacity to answer, quickly and credibly, “is this organization real, healthy and doing what it says?” Most of the sector can’t do that at scale today: about two-thirds of U.S. private foundations hold under $1 million in assets, and few have dedicated diligence staff.

2. Your grantee list becomes someone else’s due diligence

A first-time donor with $50 million and no staff will look for shortcuts. The most common one is following established funders: if a respected foundation has funded an organization for five years, that’s a signal. Expect more inbound questions about who you fund and why, and more co-funding requests. Funders whose grantmaking is easy to read will have outsized influence on where new money goes.

3. New causes, thinner track records

AI donors are already favoring causes such as AI safety, workers displaced by automation and AI literacy. Many organizations in these areas are young and have little filing history. Some file only the postcard 990-N, so there are no financials to review. Diligence will have to rely more on other evidence: the organization’s own materials, its leadership, who else funds it and whether its claims hold up.

4. Fraud follows the money

Big, fast, emotionally appealing giving attracts bad actors. In its October 30 notice on abuse of charities, the Treasury Department’s financial-crimes unit (FinCEN) listed as a red flag a “recently established” nonprofit that “suddenly receives a significant number of donations” out of line with similar organizations. Look-alike names, organizations with no real presence, and new nonprofits with sudden inflows are exactly the risks a wave of new, inexperienced donors creates.

What to do now

  1. Make your grantmaking easy to read. Publish your grantee list, focus areas and the reasoning behind multi-year relationships. If new money is going to follow you anyway, give it something accurate to follow.
  2. Set a fast-lane diligence standard. Decide what you can check in ten minutes (IRS status, sanctions lists, recent filings, state registration) versus what needs ten days. A fast first check shouldn’t replace a real review, but it shouldn’t be skipped because a review takes too long.
  3. Watch new organizations continuously, not once. A clean check at approval says little about the day of payment. Status changes, leadership turnover and revocations happen between grant cycles.
  4. Build relationships with DAF sponsors. Platforms like Daffy will sit between new donors and nonprofits. Their grant-recommendation flows, and the guidance they give donors, will shape where the money goes.
  5. Plan for co-funding. Decide in advance how you’ll respond when a new donor wants to “follow” your grant: what you’ll share, and on what terms.

The AI wealth wave is a real opportunity for the sector. But money doesn’t turn into impact on its own. In the next few years, what limits impact won’t be how much capital exists. It will be how well the sector can find, check and fund good work, quickly.
